# Middle East and Gulf — UAE and Saudi Arabia

## Dubai, UAE

**Halal food and Islamic culture:** Halal food is the norm across the whole city, the adhan sounds five times a day, and a masjid is never far away—from the elegant Jumeirah Masjid to prayer rooms in every mall.

**Calm:** Tranquility & Peace 3/10 · Healing 3/10

**Character:** Traditional 3/10 · Historic & Vintage 3/10 · Cultural 8/10 · Modern 10/10

**Energy:** Busy & Loud 9/10 · Too Much Tourism 9/10

Dubai is dazzling—ultra-modern, high-tech, polished, and full of ambition. Glass towers rise straight out of the desert, highways hum through the night, and the whole city seems built for the future. Yet the old Dubai still breathes along the Creek, where wooden abras cross the water and spice sellers call out from shaded lanes.

The pace is quick and cosmopolitan—more than eight in ten residents come from abroad, and you hear dozens of languages in a single afternoon. Dubai can be expensive, especially for hotels and nights out, but it is often not quite as costly as London or New York. Metro rides, street food, and Creek-side cafés keep it manageable.

Food is one of Dubai's greatest pleasures. You can eat your way around the world—Lebanese, Indian, Pakistani, Japanese, Italian, Thai, and beyond—and nearly all of it is halal. Masjids are everywhere, the adhan drifts over the skyline five times a day, and finding a prayer room in a mall is never hard. Summer is fiercely hot; November to March is the sweet spot.

10. Enjoy dinner by the water in Dubai Marina as the towers light up.
11. Step inside the Museum of the Future, as striking outside as it is within.
12. Visit Global Village for food, crafts, and pavilions from dozens of countries (open roughly October to May).

19. Eat your way around the world—Lebanese mezze, Pakistani karahi, Japanese ramen, Thai curries—almost all of it halal.
20. Take a sunset dhow cruise along the Creek or the Marina with dinner on board.

## Abu Dhabi, UAE

**Halal food and Islamic culture:** Virtually all food is halal, and Islamic culture sits at the heart of the capital, crowned by the breathtaking Sheikh Zayed Grand Masjid.

**Calm:** Tranquility & Peace 6/10 · Healing 5/10

**Character:** Traditional 5/10 · Historic & Vintage 4/10 · Cultural 9/10 · Modern 9/10

**Energy:** Busy & Loud 6/10 · Too Much Tourism 6/10

Abu Dhabi is Dubai's calmer, more stately older sibling—the capital, and it carries itself like one. Wide boulevards, manicured parks, and a long, breezy Corniche give the city a spacious, unhurried feel. Here, culture leads: world-class museums on Saadiyat Island, presidential palaces open to visitors, and the luminous white domes of the Sheikh Zayed Grand Masjid, one of the most beautiful masjids on earth.

Life moves at a gentler pace than in Dubai, with families strolling the waterfront at dusk and weekends spent on islands, beaches, and desert drives. Costs are broadly similar to Dubai—cheaper than London or New York, though luxury hotels and theme parks add up quickly.

The food scene mixes Emirati tradition with the whole Arab and South Asian world. Try machboos, harees, and sweet luqaimat, then grill fresh hammour from the Mina fish market. Halal food is the norm everywhere. Expect searing summers; October to April is ideal.

## Sharjah, UAE

**Halal food and Islamic culture:** Halal food is everywhere, and Sharjah is the UAE's most heritage-proud emirate for Islamic culture—home to the Museum of Islamic Civilization and hundreds of masjids.

**Calm:** Tranquility & Peace 6/10 · Healing 4/10

**Character:** Traditional 7/10 · Historic & Vintage 7/10 · Cultural 9/10 · Modern 6/10

**Energy:** Busy & Loud 6/10 · Too Much Tourism 3/10

Sharjah is the UAE's cultural heart—quieter, more traditional, and proudly rooted in its Islamic and Arab heritage. A UNESCO World Book Capital and Islamic Culture Capital, it has more museums than any other emirate, beautifully restored old quarters, and a strong sense of family life.

It is also conservative by Gulf standards: alcohol is not sold, modest dress is expected, and the city keeps a gentle, unhurried rhythm. For many Muslim travellers, that is exactly the appeal. Sharjah is noticeably cheaper than Dubai next door—hotels, rents, and meals often cost a good deal less—and it sits just a short drive (traffic permitting) from Dubai's attractions.

Food here is simple and satisfying: shawarma and grilled meats from Syrian and Lebanese joints, Pakistani and Indian curries, and fresh fish from the Jubail market. All of it is halal. Beyond the city, Sharjah stretches to the mountains and the east coast at Khor Fakkan. As across the region, summers are very hot.

12. Meet sharks and rays at the Sharjah Aquarium beside the Sharjah Maritime Museum.
13. Wander the art installations and butterfly house on Al Noor Island.
14. Take a day trip to Khor Fakkan for its beach, mountains, and the cascading Al Rafisah Dam.
15. Spot kingfishers and mangroves at Kalba on the east coast.
16. Read, relax, and admire the architecture at the House of Wisdom library.

## AlUla, Saudi Arabia

**Halal food and Islamic culture:** All food is halal, and daily life follows the rhythm of prayer, with masjids in every town and village across the valley.

**Calm:** Tranquility & Peace 9/10 · Healing 9/10

**Character:** Traditional 7/10 · Historic & Vintage 10/10 · Cultural 8/10 · Modern 5/10

**Energy:** Busy & Loud 2/10 · Too Much Tourism 5/10

**Heritage (old cities, palaces, monuments):** Hegra

AlUla feels almost otherworldly—an ancient desert valley of sandstone towers, palm oases, and silence so deep you can hear the wind move across the rock. For thousands of years it sat on the incense route, and the Nabataeans carved monumental tombs here, at Hegra, Saudi Arabia's first UNESCO World Heritage Site.

The pace is slow and spacious, ruled by sunrise, sunset, and starlight. AlUla is not cheap: luxury desert resorts and guided site tours put it closer to a premium safari than a budget trip, though simpler guesthouses and farm stays do exist. October to March is the best time to go—summer days are intensely hot.

Food leans on the oasis: sweet AlUla dates, citrus, and slow-cooked Saudi dishes such as kabsa, jareesh, and matazeez, served with cardamom-scented Saudi coffee. Halal food is a given, and the call to prayer echoing off the cliffs at dusk is unforgettable.

### 20 Things to Do in AlUla

1. Explore Hegra and stand before Qasr al-Farid, the great lone tomb carved into a single rock.
2. Watch the sunset at Elephant Rock (Jabal AlFil), then linger in the lounge seating beneath it.
3. Wander the mud-brick lanes of AlUla Old Town, once home to hundreds of houses.
4. See Maraya, the mirrored building that reflects the canyon around it.
5. Visit Dadan, capital of the ancient Dadanite and Lihyanite kingdoms.
6. Read the inscriptions of Jabal Ikmah, often called an "open-air library."
7. Take in the vast volcanic landscape from the Harrat Viewpoint.
8. Float over the valley in a hot-air balloon during the cooler months.
9. Stargaze at Gharameel, where rock spires rise into one of the clearest night skies in Arabia.
10. Cycle or walk the Oasis Heritage Trail through palm groves and old farms.
11. Climb a via ferrata route on AlUla's sandstone cliffs.
12. See the old Hejaz Railway station at Hegra, part of the line built to carry pilgrims.
13. Find the Tantora, the old sundial in AlUla Old Town that once marked the start of the winter planting season.
14. Ride a camel or horse through the desert canyons.
15. Browse galleries and cafés in the AlJadidah Arts District.
16. Visit a date farm in the oasis and taste fresh AlUla dates with Saudi coffee.
17. Try Saudi dishes such as kabsa, jareesh, and matazeez at a local restaurant.
18. Stay overnight in a desert resort tucked into the canyons of Ashar Valley.
19. See large-scale desert art at Desert X AlUla when the exhibition is running.
20. Take a day trip to Khaybar to see its historic oasis and ancient fortifications.

## Jeddah, Saudi Arabia

**Halal food and Islamic culture:** Halal food is everywhere, and Islamic culture runs deep—Jeddah is the gateway for pilgrims heading to Makkah, with centuries-old masjids in historic Al-Balad.

**Calm:** Tranquility & Peace 5/10 · Healing 4/10

**Character:** Traditional 7/10 · Historic & Vintage 8/10 · Cultural 8/10 · Modern 7/10

**Energy:** Busy & Loud 8/10 · Too Much Tourism 5/10

Jeddah is Saudi Arabia's most open-hearted city—a Red Sea port where pilgrims, traders, and travellers have mingled for centuries. For many Muslims, it is the gateway to Makkah, and that sense of welcome runs deep. Its old quarter, Al-Balad, is a UNESCO-listed maze of coral-stone houses with carved wooden balconies.

The mood is relaxed and sociable: families picnic along the Corniche, sea breezes cool the evenings, and the city comes alive late at night, especially during Ramadan. Jeddah is generally cheaper than Dubai—hotels can be good value outside the Hajj and Umrah peaks, and eating out is very affordable.

The food is a highlight. Hejazi breakfasts of foul and warm tamees bread, flaky mutabbaq, and saleeg rice share the table with Yemeni, Indonesian, and South Asian flavours brought by generations of pilgrims. And no visit is complete without a box of Albaik fried chicken. Summers are hot and humid; winter is lovely.

### 20 Things to Do in Jeddah

4. Browse spices, perfumes, and fabrics at Souq Al Alawi.
5. Walk the Jeddah Corniche at sunset with the Red Sea breeze.
6. Watch King Fahd's Fountain shoot water higher than almost any fountain on earth.
7. Visit the Al-Rahma Masjid, the "floating masjid" built out over the sea.
8. Stroll the open-air sculpture park along the Corniche, with works by famous artists.
9. Snorkel or dive the Red Sea reefs near Obhur.
10. Spend an evening at the Jeddah Waterfront, with cafés, playgrounds, and sea views.
11. Make Umrah—Makkah is about an hour's drive away (for Muslims only).
12. Eat a Hejazi breakfast of foul, tamees, and sweet tea.
13. Try mutabbaq, saleeg, and fresh seafood at local restaurants.
14. Join the queue for Albaik fried chicken, Jeddah's beloved homegrown fast food.
15. Buy the morning catch at Al-Bunt Fish Market and have it cooked nearby.
16. See contemporary Saudi art at Hayy Jameel arts complex.
17. Shop at the Mall of Arabia or the Red Sea Mall.
18. Experience Ramadan nights in Al-Balad, when the old quarter fills with lanterns, food stalls, and families.
19. Watch the Saudi Arabian Grand Prix on the Jeddah Corniche Circuit (held each spring).
20. Take a day trip to Taif to ride the Al Hada cable car and visit the rose farms.

**Halal food and Islamic culture:** Halal food is everywhere, masjids stand on almost every street, and the Islamic heritage of the Saudi heartland comes alive in historic Diriyah.

**Calm:** Tranquility & Peace 4/10 · Healing 3/10

Riyadh is a capital in motion—vast, confident, and changing at astonishing speed. Futuristic towers rise above old Najdi mud-brick forts, new boulevards and entertainment districts open every year, and the city feels like it is racing towards its own future while holding tight to its desert roots.

Life here runs late. Days are for work and shade; evenings are for cafés, family outings, and walks along the new boulevards, especially in the cooler months. Costs are moderate—cheaper than Dubai for many everyday things, though new luxury hotels and dining spots can be pricey. Summer is extremely hot and dry; October to March is far more pleasant.

Food is rooted in Najdi tradition: generous platters of kabsa, comforting jareesh, margoog stew, and endless rounds of Saudi coffee with dates. Riyadh also has one of the region's most exciting specialty coffee scenes, and every cuisine you can imagine—all halal.

## Abha, Saudi Arabia

**Halal food and Islamic culture:** Halal food is everywhere, and Islamic culture is woven into mountain life, with a masjid at the heart of every Asiri village.

**Calm:** Tranquility & Peace 9/10 · Healing 9/10

**Character:** Traditional 8/10 · Historic & Vintage 6/10 · Cultural 6/10 · Modern 4/10

**Energy:** Busy & Loud 3/10 · Too Much Tourism 3/10

Abha is Saudi Arabia's mountain escape—cool, green, and wrapped in mist for much of the year. Perched more than 2,000 metres up in the Asir highlands, it surprises almost everyone who expects only desert: terraced fields, juniper forests, and stone villages painted in bright geometric colours.

The pace is calm and outdoorsy. In summer, when the rest of the Kingdom swelters, Saudi families flock here for the mild air, so hotels fill quickly—while winter brings cold, foggy nights. Abha is affordable: rooms, meals, and transport are much cheaper than in Riyadh or Jeddah, and well below Gulf neighbours like Dubai.

The food is hearty highland fare. Look for areeka, a rich dough dish soaked in ghee and Asiri honey; haneeth, slow-cooked lamb; and fresh flatbreads from village ovens. The region's honey is prized across Arabia. Halal food is everywhere, and the old masjids and heritage villages give a real sense of southern Saudi tradition.

2. Explore Rijal Almaa's multi-storey stone houses and its local heritage museum.
3. Stand near the summit of Jabal Sawda, Saudi Arabia's highest peak.
4. See the colourful Al Qatt Al Asiri wall art, a women's tradition recognised by UNESCO.
5. Visit the Al Muftaha Arts Village, home to galleries and artists' studios.
6. Tour Shada Palace, a traditional mud-and-stone palace turned museum.
7. Browse the Tuesday Market (Souq Al Thulatha) for honey, herbs, and crafts.
8. Drive up the Green Mountain (Al Jabal Al Akhdar) for night views over the city.
9. Picnic beside the reservoir at Abha Dam.
10. Walk among juniper forests in Asir National Park.
11. Spend a misty afternoon at Dalaghan Park.
12. Wander the old Al Basta heritage district and its restored houses.
13. Visit the Bin Hamsan heritage village, a cluster of traditional stone homes.
14. Take a day trip to Habala, the "hanging village" once reached only by rope ladders.
15. Drive the dramatic switchbacks of the Aqabat Shaar road through the mountains.
16. Take a day trip to Tanomah for its granite peaks and cool summer climate.
17. Try areeka with ghee and Asiri honey, and haneeth lamb.
18. Buy Asiri honey straight from local beekeepers.
19. Hike the trails around Al Soudah through clouds and juniper.
20. Escape the summer heat in Abha's cool mountain air, when the city fills with Saudi families.

## Al Ahsa, Saudi Arabia

**Halal food and Islamic culture:** Halal food is everywhere, and Al Ahsa is one of Arabia's earliest Muslim lands—home to Jawatha Masjid, where one of the first Jumu'ah prayers after Madinah was held.

**Calm:** Tranquility & Peace 8/10 · Healing 8/10

**Character:** Traditional 9/10 · Historic & Vintage 9/10 · Cultural 7/10 · Modern 4/10

**Energy:** Busy & Loud 3/10 · Too Much Tourism 2/10

**Heritage (old cities, palaces, monuments):** oasis

Al Ahsa is an oasis on an extraordinary scale—one of the largest palm oases in the world, with millions of date palms fed by ancient springs. A UNESCO World Heritage Site, it has been farmed and settled for thousands of years, and its towns of Hofuf and Al Mubarraz still carry the rhythm of an older Arabia.

Life here is slow, warm, and deeply local. You will meet tailors stitching bishts by hand, potters at work in the caves of Al Qarah, and farmers tending dates as their grandparents did. It is one of the most affordable places to visit in Saudi Arabia—far cheaper than Riyadh or the coastal cities. Summers are brutally hot, so visit between November and March.

Food centres on the oasis harvest: Khalas dates, Hasawi red rice, and generous rice-and-meat dishes. Islamic heritage runs deep too—Jawatha Masjid is among the earliest masjid sites in the region, where Friday prayer was first held outside Madinah.

### 20 Things to Do in Al Ahsa

1. Explore the Al Ahsa Oasis, a UNESCO World Heritage Site with millions of palm trees.
2. Walk through the cool, wind-carved caves of Jabal Al Qarah.
3. Watch potters at work in the traditional workshops of Al Qarah.
4. Visit Ibrahim Palace, an Ottoman-era fort with a domed masjid in Hofuf.
5. Stand at the site of Jawatha Masjid, one of the earliest masjids in Arabia.
6. Browse the covered alleys of Al Qaisariya Souq, one of the oldest markets in the Gulf.
7. Shop at Hofuf's Thursday Market (Souq Al Khamis) for dates, spices, and crafts.
8. See Bayt Al Bay'ah, the historic house where Al Ahsa pledged allegiance to King Abdulaziz.
9. Visit Sahood Palace, the restored fort in Al Mubarraz.
10. Watch master tailors hand-stitch the gold edges of a traditional bisht.
11. Taste the famous Khalas dates, especially during the late-summer harvest.
12. Try Hasawi red rice and local rice-and-meat dishes.
13. See Al Asfar Lake, where water meets golden sand dunes.
14. Bathe or picnic by the natural spring at Ain Najm.
15. Relax in the shade of Al Ahsa National Park.
16. Take a day trip to Uqair, the historic port and fort on the Gulf coast.
17. Visit a working palm farm and drink Saudi coffee among the trees.
18. Wander the old Al Kut district of Hofuf.
19. Picnic in Jawatha Park among the palms near the masjid site.
20. Join the crowds at the Al Ahsa Dates Festival during harvest season.

## Dammam, Saudi Arabia

**Halal food and Islamic culture:** Halal food is everywhere, and masjids fill every neighbourhood of Dammam, Khobar, and Dhahran, with the adhan marking the rhythm of the day.

**Calm:** Tranquility & Peace 6/10 · Healing 4/10

**Energy:** Busy & Loud 6/10 · Too Much Tourism 2/10

Dammam is the easygoing hub of Saudi Arabia's Eastern Province—a breezy Gulf city that grew up alongside the oil industry and still feels practical, modern, and family-focused. Together with neighbouring Al Khobar and Dhahran, it forms one sprawling metro area of corniches, malls, and seaside parks.

The pace is relaxed. Evenings are spent on the waterfront, weekends at the beach or across the causeway in Bahrain. Dammam is good value—generally cheaper than Riyadh or Jeddah, and noticeably cheaper than Dubai or Doha. Summers are hot and very humid; winter is mild and pleasant.

Being on the Gulf, seafood is the star: hamour, safi, and shrimp, often served with spiced rice. Khobar has a lively café scene and restaurants from across Asia and the Arab world, all halal. Nearby Qatif and Tarut Island offer glimpses of older coastal life, and the world-class Ithra cultural centre in Dhahran is worth the trip alone.

# Middle East and Gulf — Qatar, Oman, Bahrain, Kuwait, Jordan and Azerbaijan

## Doha, Qatar

**Halal food and Islamic culture:** Halal food is everywhere, and Islamic culture shines through the grand Imam Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab Masjid and the world-class Museum of Islamic Art.

**Calm:** Tranquility & Peace 6/10 · Healing 5/10

**Character:** Traditional 5/10 · Historic & Vintage 4/10 · Cultural 9/10 · Modern 9/10

**Energy:** Busy & Loud 6/10 · Too Much Tourism 5/10

Doha is polished, purposeful and quietly grand. A gleaming West Bay skyline rises across the water from the curve of the Corniche, while the old heart of the city—Souq Waqif, with its mud-rendered walls and lantern-lit lanes—keeps one foot firmly in the Gulf's trading past. World-class museums sit beside dhows and falcon shops, and the whole city feels calm, orderly and very safe.

Life here moves at an easy, air-conditioned pace for much of the year, and the evenings come alive once the heat breaks. Doha is not cheap—hotels and dining out sit close to Dubai prices—but the metro is excellent value and many museums are free or modestly priced. From October to April the weather is gorgeous; summer is fiercely hot and humid.

The food scene is a delight, and everything is halal. Try Qatari machboos, harees and sweet luqaimat, sip cardamom-spiced karak tea, and explore a huge spread of Lebanese, Iranian, Indian and Yemeni kitchens.

### 20 Things to Do in Doha

1. Stroll the Corniche at sunset and watch the West Bay towers light up across the water.
2. Explore the Museum of Islamic Art, I. M. Pei's masterpiece, and its treasures spanning fourteen centuries.
3. Relax in MIA Park beside the museum and see Richard Serra's towering sculpture "7" on the waterfront.
4. Wander the lanes of Souq Waqif for spices, oud perfume, textiles and traditional crafts.
5. Step into the Falcon Souq and the nearby Falcon Hospital to see Qatar's beloved birds up close.
6. Visit the National Museum of Qatar, Jean Nouvel's dramatic building inspired by the desert rose.
7. Pray at or admire the Imam Muhammad ibn Abdul Wahhab Masjid, Qatar's state masjid with its sea of sandstone domes.
8. Look up at the spiral minaret of the Abdulla Bin Zaid Al Mahmoud Islamic Cultural Center, formerly known as Fanar.
9. Spend an evening at Katara Cultural Village, with its tiled Katara Masjid and beach.
10. Wander the marinas and Venetian-style Qanat Quartier of The Pearl-Qatar.

**Halal food and Islamic culture:** Halal food is standard across Türkiye, and village masjids in Göreme, Ürgüp, Avanos, and Nevşehir call the faithful to prayer five times a day.

**Calm:** Tranquility & Peace 9/10 · Healing 9/10

**Character:** Traditional 7/10 · Historic & Vintage 9/10 · Cultural 7/10 · Modern 2/10

**Energy:** Busy & Loud 3/10 · Too Much Tourism 8/10

Cappadocia feels like another planet. Soft volcanic rock has been carved by wind and time into fairy chimneys, honey-coloured valleys, and cliffs riddled with ancient cave homes and churches. Göreme, the little town at its heart, wakes before dawn as hundreds of hot air balloons drift silently into the pink sky—one of the most unforgettable sights anywhere.

Life here is slow and gentle. Days are for hiking valleys, exploring underground cities, and sitting on cave-hotel terraces with a glass of tea. It is more affordable than Istanbul for food and everyday costs, though balloon flights and boutique cave hotels can be pricey. Summers are hot and busy, winters can bring snow-dusted valleys and frequent balloon cancellations due to wind.

Local food is hearty and rustic: testi kebab cooked in a sealed clay pot, mantı dumplings, and the cured pastırma of nearby Kayseri.

### 20 Things to Do in Cappadocia

1. Float above the fairy chimneys on a sunrise hot air balloon flight.

3. Explore the Göreme Open-Air Museum and its rock-cut Byzantine churches, including the vivid Dark Church.
4. Stay at least one night in a traditional cave hotel.
5. Hike through Love Valley among its towering rock pillars.
6. Walk the Rose Valley and Red Valley trails and stay for the glowing sunset.
7. Climb Uçhisar Castle, the highest point in the region, for sweeping valley views.
8. Descend into the multi-level Derinkuyu Underground City.
9. Explore the narrow tunnels and chambers of Kaymaklı Underground City.
10. Visit Paşabağ (Monks Valley) to see its mushroom-capped fairy chimneys.

12. Wander the abandoned cave dwellings of the Zelve Open-Air Museum.
13. Take a day trip to the Ihlara Valley and hike beside the Melendiz River.

Antalya is sunshine and sea—a bright, breezy Mediterranean city framed by the dramatic Taurus Mountains. Pamukkale—"cotton castle" in Turkish—is one of nature's strangest and most beautiful creations. Mineral-rich hot springs have spilled down the hillside for thousands of years, leaving brilliant white travertine terraces filled with pale blue pools. Above them sits Hierapolis, a Roman spa city whose theatre, tombs, and colonnaded streets still crown the ridge.

Pamukkale village itself is small, quiet, and simple—a handful of guesthouses, family-run restaurants, and fields at the foot of the terraces. Most visitors come on a rushed day trip, but staying overnight lets you see the terraces glow at sunset when the crowds have gone. It is very affordable, far cheaper than Istanbul or the coastal resorts. Summer is hot and the white rock is dazzling, so arrive early.

Food is honest and homely: gözleme, village breakfasts, and in nearby Denizli, its famous oven-cooked Denizli kebabı.

Baa Atoll is the Maldives at its most wild and precious. The manta season peaks with the southwest monsoon, roughly June to November, when the sea can be rougher but the plankton blooms.

## Kozhikode, India

**Halal food and Islamic culture:** Halal food is everywhere, and Kozhikode is the heart of Malabar's Mappila Muslim heritage, with the 14th-century Mishkal Masjid at Kuttichira.

**Calm:** Tranquility & Peace 6/10 · Healing 6/10

**Character:** Traditional 9/10 · Historic & Vintage 8/10 · Cultural 9/10 · Modern 4/10

**Energy:** Busy & Loud 7/10 · Too Much Tourism 2/10

Kozhikode—long known as Calicut—is a city of spices, sea breezes, and stories. For centuries, Arab, Persian, and Chinese traders came here for pepper, and Vasco da Gama landed on its shores in 1498. That long history has given it a warm, cosmopolitan soul, a strong Mappila Muslim heritage, and an easy, friendly rhythm. It is also India's first UNESCO City of Literature.

Kozhikode is very affordable, and it is one of India's great food cities for Muslim travellers. Malabar cuisine is rich and generous: fragrant Kozhikode biryani made with short-grain kaima rice, pathiri rice flatbreads, mussels fried in spice, and the famous Kozhikode halwa sold on S.M. Street. Evenings are for snacks and Sulaimani tea by the beach. The heavy southwest monsoon arrives around June and lasts into September, turning the countryside lush and green.

### 20 Things to Do in Kozhikode

1. Visit the Mishkal Masjid in Kuttichira, a centuries-old wooden masjid that survived a Portuguese attack.
2. Walk the lanes of Kuttichira to see its old Muslim tharavad homes and the historic Jumu'ah Masjid.
3. Watch the sunset at Kozhikode Beach with a paper cone of spicy pickled fruit.
4. Buy colourful Kozhikode halwa on S.M. Street, the old "Sweetmeat Street."
5. Visit Kappad Beach, where Vasco da Gama first landed in India.
6. Eat Kozhikode biryani at Paragon, one of the city's most beloved restaurants.
7. Try the famous beef biryani at Rahmath Hotel.
8. Taste Malabar snacks like unnakaya, pazham nirachathu, and kallummakaya (spiced mussels).
9. Sip Sulaimani—spiced black tea with lemon—at a seaside tea stall.
10. Explore Valiyangadi, the old wholesale market fragrant with spices and dried fish.
11. See how traditional wooden uru ships are built at Beypore.
12. Walk the long breakwater at Beypore Beach at sunset.
13. Watch migratory birds at the Kadalundi Bird Sanctuary.
14. Hike to the Thusharagiri Waterfalls in the Western Ghats foothills.
15. Visit the Pazhassi Raja Museum and the nearby Krishna Menon Art Gallery.
16. Relax around Mananchira Square, a green park built around an old royal pond.
17. Enjoy the forested reservoir landscape around Kakkayam Dam.
18. Take a day trip to Wayanad to see the ancient carvings of Edakkal Caves.
19. Try fresh pathiri with chicken curry or fish curry for a classic Malabar meal.
20. Visit during the monsoon to see Kerala's countryside at its most vibrant green.

## Lakshadweep, India

**Halal food and Islamic culture:** Lakshadweep is almost entirely Muslim, so halal food is everywhere and every island has its masjids and a gentle rhythm shaped by prayer.

**Calm:** Tranquility & Peace 10/10 · Healing 10/10

**Character:** Traditional 9/10 · Historic & Vintage 4/10 · Cultural 7/10 · Modern 1/10

**Energy:** Busy & Loud 1/10 · Too Much Tourism 1/10

Lakshadweep is India's secret paradise—a scattering of coral islands and turquoise lagoons in the Arabian Sea. Coconut palms lean over white sand, the water is impossibly clear, and village life is calm, simple, and deeply rooted. The islanders are almost entirely Muslim, and masjids sit at the heart of every community.

It is not a casual trip. All visitors need an entry permit from the Lakshadweep Administration, and only certain islands are open to tourism—foreigners are mainly limited to places like Agatti, Bangaram, and Kadmat, with a few more open to Indian citizens. Access is by flights into Agatti or ships from Kochi. Because accommodation is limited, trips can cost more than mainland India, but daily life and local food are inexpensive. The cuisine is island-simple: tuna in every form, including dried and smoked mas, octopus fry, coconut-rich curries, and sweet kilanji. The monsoon, roughly May to September, brings rough seas.

### 20 Things to Do in Lakshadweep

1. Snorkel the coral gardens in the shallow lagoon around Agatti.
2. Spend a few days on Bangaram, a teardrop-shaped island surrounded by reef.
3. Camp on the tiny, near-deserted island of Thinnakara.
4. Learn to scuba dive in the lagoon of Kadmat Island.
5. Visit the Ujra Masjid in Kavaratti, known for its beautifully carved ceiling.
6. See the reef life at the Marine Aquarium in Kavaratti.
7. Climb the historic lighthouse on Minicoy for views of the lagoon.
8. Experience Minicoy's distinct culture, including its Mahl language and traditional Lava dance.
9. Explore Kalpeni's lagoon and the nearby islets of Tilakkam, Pitti, and Cheriyam.
10. Take a glass-bottom boat ride over the coral reef.
11. Kayak across the shallow, crystal-clear lagoons.
12. Swim with sea turtles grazing on the seagrass beds.
13. Sail from Kochi to the islands on one of the passenger ships for a true voyage.
14. Watch fishermen bring in the day's tuna using traditional pole-and-line methods.
15. Watch a performance of Kolkali or Parichakali, traditional island dances.
16. Learn about coconut and coir crafts, the backbone of island life.
17. Taste mas, the dried and smoked tuna that flavours much of island cooking.
18. Try octopus fry, tuna curry, and sweet kilanji pancakes.
19. Watch the sun set over the lagoon from the beach.
20. Stargaze over the open sea, far from any city lights.

## Hyderabad, India

**Halal food and Islamic culture:** Halal food is everywhere in the Old City, and Hyderabad is rich in Islamic heritage, from the Charminar to the grand Makkah Masjid built by the Qutb Shahi rulers.

**Calm:** Tranquility & Peace 3/10 · Healing 4/10

**Character:** Traditional 9/10 · Historic & Vintage 10/10 · Cultural 10/10 · Modern 8/10

**Energy:** Busy & Loud 9/10 · Too Much Tourism 5/10

**Heritage (old cities, palaces, monuments):** Charminar, Golconda Fort

Hyderabad is a city of two worlds. The Old City is a swirl of bazaars, minarets, and centuries of Nizami grandeur, while the gleaming tech districts of HITEC City look firmly toward the future. Between them lies a city that is proud, hospitable, and absolutely serious about its food.

It is very affordable, even compared with other major Indian cities. For Muslim travellers, Hyderabad is a joy: the Qutb Shahi and Nizam dynasties left behind magnificent masjids and palaces, and a rich Deccani culinary tradition. Hyderabadi dum biryani is world-famous, cooked slowly in sealed pots. In Ramadan, the city comes alive at night with steaming bowls of haleem. Mornings start with Irani chai and Osmania biscuits, and desserts like qubani ka meetha and double ka meetha round off every feast. Summers are very hot, so the cooler months from November to February are ideal.

### 20 Things to Do in Hyderabad

1. Climb the Charminar, the city's iconic 16th-century monument.
2. Pray at the Mecca Masjid, one of India's largest masjids.
3. Shop for lacquer bangles in the glittering lanes of Laad Bazaar.
4. Explore Chowmahalla Palace, the former seat of the Nizams.
5. Climb Golconda Fort and stay for the evening sound-and-light show.
6. Wander among the domed Qutb Shahi Tombs.
7. See the vast collection of the Salar Jung Museum.
8. Eat Hyderabadi dum biryani at a famous spot like Shadab or Paradise.
9. Try haleem during Ramadan, when stalls across the city serve it through the night.
10. Sip Irani chai with Osmania biscuits at Nimrah Café beside the Charminar.
11. Dine at Falaknuma Palace, the Nizam's hilltop palace now run as a heritage hotel.
12. Take a boat to the giant Buddha statue in Hussain Sagar Lake.
13. See the intricate marble work of the Paigah Tombs.
14. Spend a day at Ramoji Film City, one of the world's largest film studio complexes.
15. Shop for Hyderabad pearls along Pathergatti.
16. Visit the Spanish Masjid in Begumpet, with its unusual Moorish-style spires.
17. Explore the Nizam's Museum at Purani Haveli.
18. Taste qubani ka meetha (apricot dessert) and double ka meetha (bread pudding).
19. Pick up a box of fruit biscuits from Karachi Bakery.
20. Watch the sunset from the Taramati Baradari pavilion near Golconda.

## Lucknow, India

**Halal food and Islamic culture:** Halal food is everywhere in Chowk and Aminabad, and Lucknow's Nawabi Islamic heritage shines at the Bara Imambara and its Asfi Masjid.

**Calm:** Tranquility & Peace 5/10 · Healing 5/10

**Character:** Traditional 9/10 · Historic & Vintage 10/10 · Cultural 10/10 · Modern 5/10

**Energy:** Busy & Loud 8/10 · Too Much Tourism 3/10

**Heritage (old cities, palaces, monuments):** Bara Imambara

Lucknow is elegant and gracious—the City of Nawabs, famous for its tehzeeb, or refined courtesy. People still say "pehle aap" ("after you"), poetry is cherished, and grand imambaras and gateways recall the golden age of the Awadh court. The city moves at a measured, dignified pace, with old-world charm around every corner.

It is very affordable, and it is a dream for food lovers. Lucknow's Awadhi cuisine is among the finest Muslim culinary traditions anywhere: melt-in-the-mouth galouti kebabs, slow-cooked nihari with kulcha, delicate Lucknowi biryani, saffron-scented sheermal bread, and creamy kulfi. In winter, street vendors serve makhan malai, a cloud-light saffron dessert. The old quarters of Chowk and Aminabad are full of historic eateries, and the city is also famous for chikankari embroidery. Summers are extremely hot, so October to March is the most comfortable time to visit.

### 20 Things to Do in Lucknow

1. Explore the Bara Imambara and get lost in its famous Bhool Bhulaiya maze.
2. Visit the Asfi Masjid inside the Bara Imambara complex.
3. Walk beneath the Rumi Darwaza, the city's grand ceremonial gateway.
4. See the chandeliers and calligraphy of the Chota Imambara.
5. Photograph the Hussainabad Clock Tower and the unfinished Satkhanda tower nearby.
6. Pray at the Jama Masjid near Hussainabad.
7. Eat galouti kebabs at Tunday Kababi, a Lucknow legend.
8. Try nihari and kulcha at Raheem's in Chowk.
9. Taste Lucknowi biryani at Idris in the old city.
10. Enjoy makhan malai from street vendors in Chowk on winter mornings.
11. Shop for chikankari embroidery in Chowk and Aminabad.
12. Stroll along Hazratganj, the city's colonial-era shopping street.
13. Explore the ruins of the British Residency, scarred by the siege of 1857.
14. Visit the vast Ambedkar Memorial Park, built in red sandstone.
15. Try sheermal, kakori kebabs, and a sweet Lucknowi paan after dinner.
16. Cool off with kulfi at Prakash Kulfi in Aminabad.
17. Admire La Martinière College, built as the palatial home of Claude Martin.
18. Visit the ruins of Dilkusha Kothi, an old nawabi hunting lodge.
19. See the historic Chhatar Manzil, a riverside palace of the nawabs.
20. Walk through Sikandar Bagh, a garden linked to the events of 1857.

# East Africa

## Zanzibar City (Stone Town), Tanzania

**Halal food and Islamic culture:** Zanzibar is almost entirely Muslim, so halal food is everywhere, and Stone Town's Swahili-Omani Islamic culture lives in its many masjids and Qur'an schools.

**Calm:** Tranquility & Peace 7/10 · Healing 8/10

**Character:** Traditional 9/10 · Historic & Vintage 9/10 · Cultural 9/10 · Modern 2/10

**Energy:** Busy & Loud 6/10 · Too Much Tourism 7/10

**Heritage (old cities, palaces, monuments):** Stone Town

Stone Town is a labyrinth of history. Narrow alleys twist between coral-stone houses, carved wooden doors hide quiet courtyards, and the call to prayer drifts across rooftops where Swahili, Arab, Persian, Indian, and European influences meet. Once the seat of the Omani sultans, it is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and it moves to its own gentle rhythm.

Zanzibar is mostly Muslim, so halal food is easy to find and the island feels welcoming for Muslim visitors—dress modestly in town, especially during Ramadan. It is moderately priced: local meals are cheap, while beach resorts and tours can be expensive. Food is a highlight, shaped by the spice trade: pilau, biryani, coconut curries, fresh seafood, and the famous Forodhani night market with its "Zanzibar pizza" and urojo soup. The long rains from roughly March to May are the quietest time to visit.

### 20 Things to Do in Stone Town

1. Lose yourself in the alleys of Stone Town, looking out for its famous carved wooden doors.
2. Eat Zanzibar pizza, seafood skewers, and sugarcane juice at the Forodhani Gardens night market.
3. Visit the Old Fort (Ngome Kongwe), built by the Omanis in the 17th century.
4. Explore the Palace Museum, once a residence of the Zanzibar sultans.
5. Visit the Anglican Cathedral and the moving memorial on the site of the old slave market.
6. Take a spice farm tour to see cloves, nutmeg, vanilla, and cinnamon growing.
7. Sail to Prison Island (Changuu) to meet its giant Aldabra tortoises.
8. Spot rare red colobus monkeys in Jozani Forest.
9. Wander the busy stalls of Darajani Market.
10. Take a sunset dhow cruise along the Stone Town waterfront.
11. Try urojo, also known as Zanzibar mix, a tangy local soup.
12. Eat traditional Swahili dishes at Lukmaan Restaurant.
13. See the restored Hamamni Persian Baths, built for Sultan Barghash.
14. Look out for Malindi Masjid, one of Stone Town's oldest masjids.
15. Sip strong spiced coffee with the locals at Jaws Corner.
16. Snorkel with tropical fish around Mnemba Atoll.
17. Swim off the white-sand beaches of Nungwi and Kendwa.
18. Visit Kizimkazi to see dolphins and the ancient masjid with its early-12th-century inscription.
19. Have lunch at The Rock, a restaurant perched on a rock in the sea near Michamvi.
20. Join a Safari Blue dhow trip to the sandbanks of Menai Bay.
